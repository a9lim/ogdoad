\boxed{\delta_n\ge
\left\lceil\frac{d^4}{24\cdot18^4}\right\rceil
\qquad(n\ge2),\qquad d=2^{n+1}.}

Normal closures of longer segments give the superpolynomial bound

\boxed{\log_2\delta_n

**[S] Input and notation.** Use the supplied Conway tower theorem:
\[
c_j^2+c_j=a_{j-1},\qquad
a_j=\prod_{i=0}^j c_i,\qquad a_{-1}=1,
\]
where \(c_j\) generates
\[
E_j=\mathbb F_{2^{2^{j+1}}}.
\]
Write
\[
u_n=\frac{c_n+1}{c_n},\qquad
\delta_n=\operatorname{ord}(u_n),\qquad
d=2^{n+1}.
\]
Each extension \(E_j/E_{j-1}\) is quadratic, with conjugation \(c_j\mapsto c_j+1\). Set \(E_{-1}=\mathbb F_2\).

**[D] 1. An explicit cover supplying four directions.**

Put \(h(X)=X^3+X^2\), and consider
\[
\begin{aligned}
t&=x^2+x,\\
y^2+y&=h(x),\\
z^2+z&=h(y),\\
w^2+w&=ty+t^2.
\end{aligned}                                                    \tag{1}
\]
Let
\[
K_1=\mathbb F_2(x,y),\qquad
K_2=K_1(z)=\mathbb F_2(X_2),\qquad
L=K_2(w).
\]

First, \([L:K_2]=2\). On the elliptic curve with function field \(K_1\),
\[
\operatorname{ord}_\infty(x)=-2,\qquad
\operatorname{ord}_\infty(y)=-3,\qquad
\operatorname{ord}_\infty(t)=-4.
\]
The two Artin–Schreier drivers are
\[
A=h(y),\qquad B=ty+t^2.
\]
Modulo Artin–Schreier coboundaries,
\[
B\equiv ty+t.
\]
Thus \(A\), the reduced representative of \(B\), and their sum have unique poles of orders \(9,7,9\), respectively. A function with an odd-order pole cannot be an Artin–Schreier coboundary. Their classes are therefore independent, so
\[
[L:K_1]=4,\qquad [L:K_2]=2,\qquad
[L:\mathbb F_2(t)]=16.                                      \tag{2}
\]

This also proves the original obstruction precisely: \(B\) is not a coboundary even in \(K_2\), since neither \(B\) nor \(A+B\) is one in \(K_1\). The missing coordinate \(w\) genuinely requires an extension.

Define
\[
\tau(x,y,z,w)=(x+1,\ y+x,\ z+w,\ w+y).
                                                               \tag{3}
\]
It preserves (1). The relevant identities are
\[
\begin{aligned}
h(x+1)+h(x)&=t,\\
h(y+x)+h(y)&=ty+t^2=B,\\
\tau(B)&=B+h(x).
\end{aligned}
\]
Moreover,
\[
\tau^4(x,y,z,w)=(x,y,z+1,w),\qquad \tau^8=1.
\]
The automorphism \(\rho:w\mapsto w+1\), fixing \(x,y,z\), is outside
\(\langle\tau\rangle\). Consequently \(L/\mathbb F_2(t)\) is Galois of degree \(16\), and is the normal closure of \(K_2/\mathbb F_2(t)\).

Now define
\[
f_j=\frac{\tau^j(z)+1}{\tau^j(z)},\qquad 0\le j<4.
\]
Explicitly, their denominator coordinates are
\[
z,\quad z+w,\quad z+y,\quad z+w+y+x.                         \tag{4}
\]

These four functions are multiplicatively independent modulo constants. Indeed, if \(D=\operatorname{div}(f_0)\), then
\[
\tau^4D=-D.
\]
The nonzero cyclic \(\mathbb Q[\tau]\)-module generated by \(D\) is annihilated by
\[
X^4+1=\Phi_8(X),
\]
which is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\). Its dimension is therefore exactly four. Hence \(D,\tau D,\tau^2D,\tau^3D\) are independent.

Each \(f_j\) has pole degree \(18\): the degree of \(z\) on \(X_2\) is \(9\), and \(L/K_2\) has degree two.

At the selected specialization
\[
(x,y,z)=(c_{n-2},c_{n-1},c_n),
\]
the corresponding point on \(L\) has degree \(d\), and all four values in (4) are Frobenius conjugates of \(c_n\). Thus
\[
f_j(P_L)\in\langle u_n\rangle,\qquad f_0(P_L)=u_n.
                                                               \tag{5}
\]
The specialization assertion follows from the general argument below; it does not assume \(\delta_n=F_n\).

The evaluation kernel in \(\mathbb Z^4\) consequently has index \(\delta_n\). Every nonzero relation gives a nonconstant function
\[
\prod_{j=0}^3f_j^{a_j}-1
\]
vanishing at a degree-\(d\) point, with pole degree at most
\(18\sum_j|a_j|\). Four-dimensional Minkowski yields
\[
\delta_n\ge\frac{d^4}{4!\,18^4},
\]
as claimed.

supply two independent pairs:

Thus two points do help when placed on this explicit correspondence. Their common curve retains a usable one-dimensional zero-counting argument, and their selected values lie in the same cyclic group.

**[D] 2. Normal closures give unbounded rank with controlled cost.**

For \(m\ge0\), define a generic tower over \(\mathbb F_2(t)\):
\[
x_0^2+x_0=t,\qquad
x_j^2+x_j=h(x_{j-1})\quad(1\le j\le m).
                                                               \tag{6}
\]
Let
\[
K_m=\mathbb F_2(t,x_0,\ldots,x_m),
\]
let \(L_m\) be its normal closure over \(\mathbb F_2(t)\), and put
\[
N=2^{m+1},\qquad r=2^m,\qquad
e_m=[L_m:K_m],\qquad H_m=3^m e_m.
\]

The quantitative conclusion is
\[
\boxed{
\delta_n\ge
\left\lceil
\frac{d^{\,2^m}}{(2^m)!\,H_m^{\,2^m}}
\right\rceil
\quad(0\le m\le n).
}                                                            \tag{7}
\]
Furthermore,
\[
\boxed{
H_m\le 3^m\,2^{\,2^{m+1}-m-2}.
}                                                            \tag{8}
\]

Here the relevant normal closure is over \(t=x_0^2+x_0\), not merely over \(x_0\).

*Proof of the geometric and degree assertions.*

Starting on \(\mathbb P^1_{x_0}\), each successive driver has a unique odd-order pole. Therefore every step in (6) is geometrically irreducible and quadratic, with a unique point over infinity. Inductively,
\[
[K_m:\mathbb F_2(t)]=N,\qquad
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(x_m)=-3^m.
\]
Consequently
\[
g_m=\frac{x_m+1}{x_m}
\]
has pole degree \(3^m\) on \(K_m\), and pole degree \(H_m\) on \(L_m\).

All finite places are unramified: the tower consists of monic Artin–Schreier equations with derivative one, and its normal closure remains unramified there.

At \(t=0\), every coordinate of every branch is binary. Hensel lifting puts all branches in \(\mathbb F_2[[t]]\). In particular, \(L_m\) has constant field exactly \(\mathbb F_2\).

To construct the normal closure, adjoin roots at every vertex of the binary tree. There are
\[
1+2+\cdots+2^m=N-1
\]
quadratic equations, each costing degree at most two. Hence
\[
[L_m:\mathbb F_2(t)]\le2^{N-1},
\qquad
e_m\le\frac{2^{N-1}}N,
\]
which proves (8).

*Proof of multiplicative rank \(r\).*

At \(t=1\), (6) specializes to the first \(m+1\) levels of the Conway tower. The supplied tower theorem says that this fiber of \(K_m\) is one closed point of degree \(N\).

Choose a point of \(L_m\) above it, and let \(\tau\) be its Frobenius element. On the \(N\) embeddings of \(K_m\), \(\tau\) acts as an \(N\)-cycle. The normal-closure action on these embeddings is faithful, so \(\tau\) has order exactly \(N\).

The same argument on the preceding level shows that \(\tau^r\) fixes
\(K_{m-1}\). It acts nontrivially on the last quadratic extension, so
\[
\tau^r(x_m)=x_m+1,\qquad
\tau^r(g_m)=g_m^{-1}.                                      \tag{9}
\]
For \(m=0\), this is simply the deck involution of \(x_0^2+x_0=t\).

Set \(D=\operatorname{div}(g_m)\ne0\). Equation (9) gives
\[
(\tau^r+1)D=0.
\]
Since
\[
X^r+1=\Phi_{2^{m+1}}(X)
\]
is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\), the cyclic module generated by \(D\) has dimension \(r\). Therefore
\[
g_m,\tau(g_m),\ldots,\tau^{r-1}(g_m)                       \tag{10}
\]
are multiplicatively independent modulo constants.

*Proof of selected subgroup membership and point degree.*

For \(n\ge m\), specialize
\[
x_j=c_{n-m+j},\qquad
t=t_0=a_{n-m-1}.                                          \tag{11}
\]
The element \(a_k\) generates \(E_k\): its relative trace is
\(a_{k-1}\ne0\), so it is outside \(E_{k-1}\), which contains every proper subfield of \(E_k\). Thus
\[
[\mathbb F_2(t_0):\mathbb F_2]=2^{n-m}.
\]
This also holds when \(n=m\), with \(t_0=1\).

The selected fiber of \(K_m\) over \(t_0\) is one point of relative degree
\[
[E_n:E_{n-m-1}]=N.
\]
Its Frobenius therefore acts as an \(N\)-cycle on the embeddings of \(K_m\). Faithfulness again shows that its order in the normal-closure group is exactly \(N\). Since the place is unramified, a point \(Q\) of \(L_m\) above it has residue field exactly \(E_n\), hence degree \(d\) over \(\mathbb F_2\).

Every conjugate of the top coordinate specializes to one of
\[
c_n^{\,2^{\,2^{n-m}j}},\qquad 0\le j<N.
\]
Accordingly, every function in (10) evaluates to a power of \(u_n\), and \(g_m(Q)=u_n\).

Crucially, **the fixed automorphism \(\tau\) chosen at \(t=1\) need not belong to the decomposition group at \(t_0\)**. Total inertness of the original tower fiber already puts every conjugate top-coordinate value in the same Frobenius orbit.

*Proof of the order estimate.*

Write the \(r\) functions in (10) as \(f_i\). Their evaluation map
\[
\mathbb Z^r\longrightarrow\langle u_n\rangle,
\qquad
(a_i)\longmapsto\prod_i f_i(Q)^{a_i}
\]
is surjective, so its kernel \(\Lambda\) has index \(\delta_n\).

For \(0\ne a\in\Lambda\), multiplicative independence ensures that
\[
F_a=\prod_i f_i^{a_i}-1
\]
is nonzero. It vanishes at \(Q\), giving
\[
d\le\deg_\infty(F_a)\le H_m\sum_i|a_i|.                    \tag{12}
\]
The cross-polytope \(H_m\sum_i|a_i|<d\) has volume
\[
\frac{2^r d^r}{r!\,H_m^r}.
\]
Minkowski’s theorem applied to (12) proves (7). ∎

**[D] 3. Explicit superpolynomial consequence.**

Put \(\ell=\log_2d=n+1\). From (8),
\[
H_m\le 2^{2r-2}r^{\log_2(3/2)}.
\]
Using \(r!\le r^r\) in (7) gives
\[
\log_2\delta_n
\ge r\bigl(\ell-2r+2-(\log_2 3)\log_2r\bigr).             \tag{13}
\]

For \(\ell\ge4\), choose \(r=2^m\) to be the largest power of two at most
\(\ell/4\). Then
\[
\ell/8<r\le\ell/4,\qquad m\le n.
\]
On this interval,
\[
r(\ell-2r)\ge\frac{3\ell^2}{32}.
\]
Consequently the following completely explicit bound holds for \(n\ge3\):
\[
\boxed{
\log_2\delta_n
\ge
\frac{3}{32}(n+1)^2
-\frac{\log_2 3}{4}(n+1)\log_2(n+1).
}                                                            \tag{14}
\]
The exact maximum of (7), using (8), is stronger than this simplified expression.

In particular,
\[
\frac{\log\delta_n}{\log d}\longrightarrow\infty.
\]
This proves superpolynomial growth in \(d\). It remains far below full order:
\[
F_n=2^{d/2}+1.
\]

**[D] 4. Quaternion endomorphisms: corrected annihilator and a genuine obstruction.**

For this paragraph assume \(n\ge3\), and write
\[
P=(c_{n-1},c_n),\qquad R=\operatorname{ord}_{E}(P),\qquad
i=\iota,\qquad h=2^{2^{n-2}}.
\]
The supplied elliptic identities give
\[
iP=sP,\qquad s=\pm h,\qquad R\mid h^2+1=F_{n-1}.
\]

Let \(A=\mathbb Z[i]\). The exact annihilator is
\[
\boxed{
I_A=\operatorname{Ann}_A(P)
=(R,i-s)
=\{a+bi:a+sb\equiv0\pmod R\}.
}
                                                               \tag{15}
\]
Thus
\[
[A:I_A]=R,\qquad
\boxed{(i-s)A\subseteq I_A.}                                \tag{16}
\]
The principal ideal on the left has norm \(F_{n-1}\).

**Gemini’s containment is reversed.** Equality in (16) holds exactly when
\(R=F_{n-1}\); that equality was not established uniformly. Its claimed large representative bound cannot be deduced from the stated annihilation relation.

**[S/D] Field of definition and full-order bookkeeping.** For this particular twist,
\[
\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb F_2}(E)
=\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb F_4}(E)=\mathbb Z[i],
\]
because \(\pi=i-1\) and \(\pi^2=-2i\) have centralizer \(\mathbb Q(i)\). All geometric endomorphisms are defined over \(\mathbb F_{16}\), since
\(\pi^4=[-4]\) is central. These use the standard Frobenius-centralizer description; see [Sutherland, Lecture 14](https://math.mit.edu/classes/18.783/2019/LectureNotes14.pdf).

For the full quaternion order \(\mathcal O\), the standard action
\(\mathcal O/R\mathcal O\simeq M_2(\mathbb Z/R)\) implies
\[
[\mathcal O:\operatorname{Ann}_{\mathcal O}(P)]=R^2,
\]
so the annihilator’s reduced ideal norm is \(R\), not an established
\(F_{n-1}\). The matrix-ring identification is given in [Voight, Theorem 42.1.9](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-56694-4_42).

**[D] Small-degree scalar-action obstruction.** If
\[
\alpha\in\mathcal O\setminus A,\qquad \alpha(P)\in\langle P\rangle,
\]
then
\[
\boxed{\deg\alpha\ge R/4>d/4.}                             \tag{17}
\]

Indeed, both \(\alpha\) and \(i\) act as scalars on \(P\), so
\[
(\alpha i-i\alpha)(P)=O.
\]
The commutator is nonzero because the centralizer of \(i\) in \(\mathcal O\) is \(A\). Its kernel contains the \(R\)-element group \(\langle P\rangle\), hence
\[
R\le\deg(\alpha i-i\alpha)\le4\deg\alpha.
\]
